## Sermon

{{< collapse summary="**Sermon Summary**">}}

Matthew 9:35–38 frames the urgent call to pray for laborers in the harvest fields. The passage portrays Jesus teaching, proclaiming the kingdom, and healing, then looking on the crowds with compassion because they were “harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.” That compassion yields a clear charge: the harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few—therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers. The text presses the church into focused intercession, not as an optional piety, but as the divinely appointed means to see gospel laborers raised and sent.

Three compelling reasons drive that charge. First, pervasive suffering—physical hunger, disease, spiritual blindness, and the reality of eternal judgment—weighs on billions who have never heard or experienced the peace of Christ. Second, the size of the harvest remains vast: a majority of the world has little or no access to the gospel, and many regions show rapid responsiveness when the message arrives. Third, a stark shortage of harvesters limits harvest work; fewer missionaries serve now than decades ago, and windows of gospel receptivity close as societies grow more affluent. Taken together, these facts create a moral and spiritual urgency: ignoring the need risks leaving multitudes unharvested.

The response required is concrete and communal. The Lord of the harvest must be asked—specific, sustained intercession for people to be called, trained, supported, and sent. Prayer must pair with willing obedience: some are called to go cross-culturally, others to send, pray, and fund; both roles share in the commission. Historical examples and recent mission stories show rapid fruit where the gospel goes, underscoring that timely sending matters. Finally, the call carries pastoral tenderness: intercession seeks souls, not acclaim; it seeks laborers so eternal suffering might be staved and nations might know Christ.

The passage closes with a practical summons: incorporate focused missionary prayer into private and corporate life, cultivate willingness to go if called, and commit to sending and sustaining those who will take the gospel to unreached places. The harvest waits; the responsibility to ask the Lord to send workers rests squarely on the people of God.

### Interpretation questions

* Jesus describes the crowds as "harassed and helpless." In what ways do people in our modern world, both locally and globally, still experience this same kind of spiritual harassment and helplessness?
* The command to "pray to the Lord of the harvest" is given in the context of a plentiful harvest and few workers. Why is prayer, rather than immediate action, presented as the first and primary response to this problem?
* The story was shared of a church that waited 148 years to send out its first missionary. What cultural or spiritual factors within a church family might lead to such a long delay in participating in global missions?

### Application questions

* The harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few. What would it look like to incorporate specific, sustained prayer for gospel workers into your daily or weekly routine? Who exactly will you begin praying for—by name or by people group?
* Physical hunger, disease, spiritual blindness, and eternal peril create a pressing moral burden. When you hear statistics about global suffering, what is your typical emotional and spiritual response? How can intercessory prayer transform that response from feeling overwhelmed to engaging in compassionate action?
* Obedience means either going cross-culturally or sending and supporting those who do. What is your current posture toward God if He were to call you or someone in your immediate family to go? Is it a fearful reluctance or a willing openness? What would need to change to make it a willing "yes"?
